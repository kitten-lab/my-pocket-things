# TheBox

i.

At the corner of Dusty Heights and Main, between two rather large and imposing, dull skyrises, sits a small curiosity of a shop, Ridgemoore Treasures.  While some geniuinely loved the hunt of good antiques, most people shared the same thought about Ridgemoores’ and about the owner, Cedric: an eccentric hoarder, nothing more. 
You see, Ridgemoore Treasures was stuffed to the brim on the inside, filled with the widest range of things you could imagine. From simple dresser drawers made of unique materials from small villages in India, to tiny little fragments of metal with rich and ridiculous lore that only a madman would believe.
	Cedric, and avid and well-known collector, was quite capable of acquiring the most unique of items, and while he made some pomp and circumstance that he was doing it for business, Rigdemoore’s Treasures stood more as a Museum of the Odd and the Beautiful, and in the later years of Cedrics life, the only place you could find Cedric, as he grew into a shut in. A bit raving mad too, by most standards.
	Amelia Ridgemoore, Niece and Former Employee of Ridgemoore’s Treasures walked through the piles of treasures, fingers running over the dust lining the tops of the dressers and bookshelves and strangely shaped statues of Eagles headed humans holding strange pillar objects (of which he had several of these pieces, as strange as that sounds).  A pang of sadness ticked at the back of her mind; sadness she would never see her uncle again, guilt that she had not done more to help him in what now would be considered his twilight years.
	In the far back of the store was an Employees Only door which led to a much smaller room. Presumably the “boss’” office, it was now a cluttered mess of boxes and bags in sky-reaching towers, bowing inward towards the center, a small bed where Cedric spend his last few years. Amelia sighed, shaking her head and the state of things. It all seemed rather sudden, the loss of her uncle, and she’d not quite come to terms with it all.
	Amelia Ridgemoore, 2 years ago, had taken the big plunge to go off from town to the Big City, join a university, get herself a higher education. She was always fiercely protective of her uncle, especially as in his declining years, but her mother assured her that Cedric would want this, and Cedric, though not very present anymore, seemed to think her departure was the right choice.
	She’d meant to go home, to see Cedric, but school was difficult and mom and dad frequently would come up and visit her, especially near holidays. There was never a solid reason to force her way back home, and she received occasional, if not unclear jibberish, letters from him confirming he was plotting along as you’d expect a dying madman to.
	She was riding her bike on the school bike paths when she got the text that Cedric was “gone” and she should come home that weekend for the funeral, to pay her respects. It struck her odd then, and it struck her odder now and she looked around the makeshift bedroom.
	As with the main room, there was an abnormally thick layer of dust. It coated everything, including the bed. But to the side of the bed was a small table made of reclaimed wood, decades old paint still gripping to it in some places. Not shockingly, this table also was covered in dust, but on it, out in the open and totally within dustable range, sat a box. This box sat, dust beneath it but not on it. It itself presenting a riddle, a pesky question in the back of the mind.
	The box, about 6” cubed, was inlayed on one side with a remarkable black stone, so dark that it almost seemed like a hole in the fabric of vision itself. The additional paneled sides depicted a range of symbolism and words Amelia assumed were in languages that outdated most of the objects in the room. Drawn to the lack of dust and the bottomless blackness, Amelia reached out, fingers grazing the vantablack surface. 
	Like a trigger, a sudden memory is recalled. 
*It is a windy fall day, mid October. Her jacket is too big, her mother says she will grow into it. She doesn’t zip it up, and the wind catches the corners. She shrugs her shoulders to keep the jacket on in the wind. The school bus is coming. Cedric is here. The wind feels too big, its roaring and its loud and her jacket feels like its going to fly away and maybe she is going to fly away. *
*Cedric is shouting. She can’t remember his words. She remembers they were fragmented. She remembers her hands **and his hands. He was reaching out to her. Trying to catch her from flying away into the storm? Or was he handing her something? The box. She remembered the box, the black material. She remembered her hands reaching out, reaching for cedric, shoulders shrugging, trying to keep the jacket on over the roaring wind. She remembered her fingers, warm and tingling, pushing through the black material, into the bottomless pit of darkness. *
*The roar of the wind became the roar of the bus and the roar of her mother. These words felt clearer, “Cedric, get back! **Amelia**, GO TO SCHOOL! The bus is here!”** Her fingers recoiled, the wind died down, the memory faded.*
Today, her fingers did not sail into the box, the blackest of blacks offered resistance. Fingers instead met the feel of polished stone, cold and glossy. Her brow furrowed as she considered the memory, a laughed to herself over the absurdity of what was obviously now the memory of a dream.
The box though. In her heart, she knew she’d seen it before. She knew this box somehow. Somehow was drawn to it. 
“Woohoo! Ameliay, earth to Amelia!” A familiar voice shot through the silence, scaring the box out of her hands. She fumbled, stepping forward a few steps to gain her balance and catch the box. She spun around to see who the intruder was, and obviously her face carried the truth of her emotions: annoyed surprise.
“Jeez, Amelia, its only me,” said Rich Michaels, Amelia’s childhood friend and a real shit for brains. She hadn’t seen him in as long as she’d not seen Cedric, since he decided to skip the whole Higher Education™ routine and stay back and take care of his ailing mother and her small town diner (“Barb’s 24 Hours”). Her expression softened to friendly disapproval, and then a broad smile, even so riddled with sadness over the loss of her uncle.
“I know, he was a really cool guy,” Rich offered in condolence as he pulled her in for a tight hug. A real pain in the ass, but he was a fantastic support system when you needed it. “Want to go to mum’s dinner and grab a bite on the house? Catch up?”
Amelia had had enough of the dust drenched memories of her uncles collections and nodded, tossing the box into her sachel. “Taking this with me,” she said. “to remember him by.”

ii.
They rode they bikes about 2 miles up the main street. Time always seemed to be longer when you are away. Stores come and go. Places open and placed close. Kids grow up. Things felt the same as they passed familiar placed, but an eerie difference coated everything. The town has moved on. Amelia had moved on.
Barb’s 24 Hour’s hadn’t changed much since she was last in town, though it was now technically Barb’s 10 hours as they scaled back on open hours as Barb’s illness grew worse. It was sad and she wasn’t sure how to comfort of help Rich, instead focusing on sucking back handfuls of French fries as he unloaded a laundry list of struggles.
“So, because of that last timble, mom has been on bedrest for the last, like, 3 months and its been hard to keep the doors open all the time,” Rich yammered on. Amelia provided occasional grunts and condolences, French fries continuing to be the space she needed between him and his grief. “And the light company says they will handle the costs for this month since they love the diner and mum’s always been prompt with her payments, but they don’t think they are going to be able to do that anymore in the future so we got to figure that out.”
Amelia swallowed the last massive handful of fries, wiping the oils from her lips on her sleeve. “I can imagine its hard,” she mustered, but it felt as inauthentic as it sounded.
“Yeah, things have moved on,” he replied, gazing out the window, its views overlooking a parking lot with very few cars and beyond that, a highway and a roadside sign promoting Jack Raven’s, a local bar and supposedly *The Originators of the True Spice Wings*. 
“And now, Cedric is gone,” she agreed. 
Some time passed. The pile of French fries and the burger on her plate slowly reduced itself to crumbs. In stark contrast, Rich’s was hardly touched. He continued to stare out the window, occasionally sharing a random fact about a school friend or neighborhood place. They tried to share pleasant news of their life: an aced college exam, a girlfriend who knows exactly the bests gifts to buy.
After a period of silence, Amelia asked, “Do you remember 6th grade, mid-October, any insanely windy days?” 
“Who the fuck remembers sixth!” he laughed, then seeing she was serious, continued, “I don’t personally. There was that time the old Holyard barn got knocked down, don’t know if that was wind or something else, but that’s around the same time, 6th or maybe 7th grade?”
Amelia nodded.
Always an interviewer and never one to leave a stone unturned, Rich continued. “What makes you ask?”
Amelia took The Box out from her bag and placed it on the center of the table, rotating it 90 degrees initially, in what she felt was “facing forward” towards Rich. “I found this box at Cedric’s,” she started. She told him about the dust, and the immense winds and the weird dream-memory.  About her arms reaching deep into the black space that was now solid stone on the box’s surface. She tapped the surface as she relayed the tale, a soft and soothing knocking, no fingers drifting in.
“Well,” Rich said sharply, tossing the napkin from his lap into his plate. “Clearly a dream.”
“Yeah, clearly,” she mused, lot in the see of itriquite details along the box’s sides. “Just seemed so vivid.”
“If it helps, I did hear something about a huge, localized wind storm that happened maybe 6 months ago. Tore down a few trees, knocked out the powerlines in the neighborhood for 3 days. Real freakish. I am not sure if its related, but I know I’d seen an article in The Daily that talked about it. Tore down the Bayberry Barn.”
Amelia grabbed the box and tossed it back into her sachal and gestured in agreement. “Yup, I’d love to read it. And maybe we can find the obituary my mom write for him, since they didn’t show me. How much do we owe for the meal?”
“On the house,” Rich said, humorless tone. “Won’t be much business left soon anyways.”

iii.

They rode they bikes further down Main and took a left on Willow. A few miles down was the old library, as well as the police center and the fire house. The surrounding area was richly wooded, with gorgeous blooming flowers and new leaves on the trees. The library hadn’t changed since she left, heck it hadn’t changed since she was a child. 
Inside the busilding was a small collection of children’s books and two larger sections, Fixtion and Non-Fiction, for the grown up books. A meek woman with horn rimmed glasses sat behind the counter, reading a book with intense focus. Amelia and Rich waiting for some indication she was going to notice them, but she seemed deeply emmeshed in her book. 
“The Practical Guide to Practicatlity!” was the title of the book and she certainly struck Amelia as a practical type of woman who would enjoy such a book about practicality. 
“A-hem” Amelia cleared her throat, jolting the poor librarian from her deep trance. As expected, she threw a tart look at her unwelcome visitors, but still was distinctly aware of the required service she was meant to provide.
“Yes, sorry. Can I help you?” she whispered.
	“Yup, looking for some help with the newsroller.”
	“You mean the *microfiche?*”

	Sometime later, Amelia and Rich were proudly seated infront of the ancient monolith of a device looking at the article from six months previous. It described a sudden wind storm, wind gusts measuring near category 5 hurricane levels, localized to a quarter mile radius between Dusty Heights and Center street, only a block away from her uncles house. Bemused quotes from bystanders seemed to feel it was one of those “mini-twisters” they’d heard about on the national news, and others thought it was something strange related to the ever-growing threat of climate change.
	Amelia continued to read on the following page where a mother was quoted, “My daughter was nearly blown away getting off the bus! Her jacket was billowing in the wind and I was just certain she was going to turn right into a kite and swirl off into the wind.” That part made Amelia laugh, but it also gave her a twisting pang of uneasiness in her gut. Sounded familiar, much like her dream.
	“Wonder if there’s any other similar events,” Rich said, kicking his feet up, one over the other, onto the desk. He leaned back in the old office chair, letting out a creaking ache of desperately needing to be oiled metal.
	“SHHHH!” came the expected Librarian response from the other room. “Feet off the table, young man!” 
	He quickly dropped his feet, and shouted back, “Can you help us search on this sucker?!”

	Three additional events had happened with similar attributed, small localized wind storms with large gusts that were not predicted by the weather, lasted a rather short amount of time, and were remarkable if only for those things. Each happened in a random month of the year, and with seemingly no explainable pattern. The oldest instance was written about near 75 years ago, “Down by the shops on Main Steet,” the article read. Still so close to the Treasures Shop. 
	“Strange, eh?” Rich said as he closed out of the final article.
	“Yep,” Amelia said, packing her notebook and items into her bookback. “I should go, my mom will likely want to see me.”
	“Your mom?” Rich asked, an expression of concern and puzzle on his face. “You finally found her?”
	“What?” 
	“Your mom. You said she went missing when you were a kid.” 
	“I.. what?” A feeling of unease passed over her. “I.. thought…”

Her mother was a lovely women with amber eyes and long blonde hair nearly white in its lightness. She went to church functions and braided her hair twice a week. Amelia would braid it for her sometimes, and they would talk about things that mothers and daughters shared, boys and school and clothing tips. 

Her mother would take her to the movies. 

Her mother joined her at every function

iv.

	She felt ill and dizzy and deeply unsure of her world. After Rich’s claim she had never known her mother, she ran home to what she knew was her home growing up. Rich followed her there. She knocked on the door and her aunt Marie answered. Aunt Marie confirmed what Rich had told her, and instantly flew into a fit of worry over the loss of Cedric and how its impacted Amelia, how Amelia was obviously so distraught that right didn’t seem like right anymore.
	“Up sure isn’t up,” Amelia bemused, stuck with a deep sense of dread and mysterious confusion.
	That night, she placed the box next to her side table. She ran her fingers again over the black darkness, and sighed to herself.
	Wasn’t it mother who has stopped Cedric that day in the wind? Well of course not, that was just a dream. But she did have a mother, didn’t she? Couldn’t she remember a single other aching memory of the woman? The more she tried to think about it, the more her head began to ache, throbbing deep behind her left eye.
	This was all too much, she thought, and opted for the safety of sleep. Instead of peace, she lay away on her back, mind crawling through the sudden bleakness of her memory.
Time passed and then it began to feel as though the walls rattled and bowed, seeming to suck inward towards her bed. The dark shadows deepened, no longer corners but bottomless chasms, her body above them and them below, taunting her to fall away from her blankets, to drift rapidly into their clutching arms, outreached blackness engulfing her. She thought of the box, of its deep onyx inlay, an invitation to come inside and yet solid to her fingertips. But she could not peel her eyes away from the growing dark corners to look at the box beside her on the table. No, here she could only sit, eyes peeled opened large, invited into the darkness.
And from the darkness, the edges begin to drip, colored drains of paint matching the colors of the world around her. Muave walls with decals of birds and classic flowers become liquidy, melting away into the darkness. She is unsure if the darkness is eating the colors, consuming the world around here, or if the blackness is melting the world, boiling it down to its base elements until nothing more is left.
In the moment, she is aware she does not care. Things do not make sense here in this tightened dream, but they do not make sense to her in the real world anymore. She wonders for a moment how the real world could even be real when she felt so sure she knew her mother and not she was so sure she did not.
The dripping continued until the world around her was nothing but the deep blackness, and she felt a comfort in the expectation to look around her and see nothing. She reaches her arms outward, risking the sinking feeling that she can be pulled in. The air is still and dry, the lack of circulation gives a sense of warmth that only a breeze can cure.
As she reaches out, the edges of the black, the corners where the darkness rose from, begin to turn light again. A new light enters at those edges, brighter then her eyes can stand and she shields her eyes from the light, squinting. The brightness grows and overtakes the shadows, and as its grows the brightness intensifies to a point where she cannot even squint to view it anymore. 
She closes her eyes.

v.

She woke with the sun. She said nothing. She moved through the world like quicksand, with a bit of a confusion. She wanted to ask Aunt Marie so many questions, but she was scared of how she was going to be seen. She could remember her mother now in literally only her dream phase, and while she had a deep feeling connection to her, she really did feel everyone was telling her the truth.
She pushed around the scrambled eggs with a fork, an unpleasant squealing sound drawing Marie’s attention to the lack of appetite.
“Oh honey,” she said, reaching out to pat her on the hand. “I know its hard. Cedric was a lot of what you had. He was your number one and I know you feel so bad you weren’t here when he went.”
Amelia nodded, continuing to push her food around. Marie gathered the plates on the table, including Amelias, with a sigh. She always tried to be a good mother to Amelia, but it was hard sometimes to not feel like she was just forcing herself into a place she just didn’t belong.
Marie dropped the plates into the sink, looking out the kitchen window into the field in the distance. The sun was finished rising, and the animals from Buckley’s farm were roaming the distance. She always liked to watch the cows as their grazed the pastures while she loaded the dishes into the new fangled dishwasher Cedric had brought back home some 60 years ago. It has a certain look of copper and strange piping, rivets and gears. She recalled one time a boy by the name of Sam or Mark, something short and simple, had come by and helped her with the groceries when Cedric had been gone on one of his collecting trips, and he’d called it *steampunk*. She didn’t really get the word choice, but she recalled he really seemed giddy to look it over and she’d made him a cup of tea and he’d eaten some of the scones he’d just delivered.
	“I miss him,” Marie said, eyes still fixed out the window. The water run from the faucet, a gentle sound. It felt soothing. “I sure do. And I know we didn’t have him for a few years, we lost him when his mind was lost, but I miss him.
	“He was a good man and he loved us. And he especially loved you and he was glad you went off to school.” Aunt Marie shut the water off and dried her hands on a chicken themed towel, fitting with the country motif of everything in the kitchen (besides that steampunk dishwasher, and the brand new digital LG Refrigerator Marie had replaced 2 years ago, when the *steampunk* one bit the dust and he was too gone to help fix it.).
	Clasping her hands on Amelia’s shoulder, she sighed, sinking back down into her chair. “We knew this day was going to come, darlin’.” 
	Amelia nodded, finally looking up and making eye-contact with with her caring aunt. She didn’t know how to seek comfort anymore, lost in a sea of unclear memories. She wanted to be held, to be understood, to find comfort. Surely, it was just the grief of loss. She fumbled to say anything back, but her Aunt just gave her a caring pat, and understanding nod of the eye and pulled her in tight for a hug.
	“I’m always here for you honey.”
	“I know aunty. Thank you…”

The hills were rolling, green and rich in a way that felt quite unnatural to her eyes, and she thought about the hue for more time then she considered possible to think of color. Soft and delicate tiny green plants struck her as similar to four leaf clovers, except they didn’t have four leaves nor were they clovers. Each *blade of grass* was instead like a small pill covered with soft green fur. The effect caused an almost velvet like sheen as the light of the day hit the edges of the hills in the distance.
The sky was a gentle range of blues to orange, the sun hovering at the rim of the horizon, unsure if it is rising or falling. There was a smell of fresh bloom in the breeze, gentle and calming. It felt and smelt exactly like spring, she thought, and she knew that it was good.
She knelt down, the ground moist with the sweet dew of the air, and touched the tiny little fuzz-like objects. They were as soft as she expected, the tiny fibers almost like a micro-fleece. At her feet, there it was. The box. How was that even possible, she’d just gone THROUGH the box, or so it seemed, to get to this place. But yet, here it was, safe and sound.
*Perhaps I can get back through it,* she considered, and immediately, despite all of her better judgement, tried to shove her hand back into the deep black opening at the top. Her hand instead slammed against the box. *Closed. *Time for a plan B.
Beyond the fields and rolling hills, she could see the formation of some kind of *path of desire*, a stretch of dirt leading through and beyond the rich green foliage. 
*Follow the yellow brick road,* Amelia clucked to herself, and follow it she did, taking The Box with her.
. . .
She walked for what felt like hours, but there was really no sense of time here. The rich orange in the sky didn’t seem the change, the sun didn’t seem to move from its place. The rich green rolling hills seemed to continue on for quite some time. Amelia rubbed her thumbs across the cool black surface as she clutched the box. She wondered how it could be she was in this place, and what it all meant. She had a strong realization that she likely was just deep in another dream. The thought was comforting.
Beyond the green fringes began the uppopping of unique flowering plants in a range of colors – pinks and yellows and pastels blues popped out of rich greens against the same orange sky. Amelia thought these must be the spring blooms that were providing such a lovely smell to the sky. 
There was a sense of enthusiasm here, despite the fact that she had no idea where she was, or if she was sleeping. She felt excited. She felt as though this could be the place that her grandfather had talked about, *the lands beyond*, he sometimes called them. She tried to remember some of his stories she told her when she was a child, bedtime stories that eventually drove him mad.  She found it difficult to remember most. In fact, she struggled to remember much in this moment.
*Strange, *she thought, and chocked the brain fog to side effects to the *travel* she underwent. *Travel* certainly didn’t feel like the right word for what had happened to her, but it was the best she could pull and she allowed herself to be comforted by the idea that her memories would become easier to remember soon enough.
Miles and miles of green passed under her feet and the sun didn’t move an inch. She soon grew tired and thirsty and though she felt ambitious and wanted to continue on, she felt the need to rest. *What I wouldn’t give for a nice place to stop, maybe find some answers, *she thought.
As if an answer from above, just beyond the next hill she saw what appeared to be a small smoke stack. Could it be a house, she thought, excited by the prospect of, at the least, finding a new place to explore. As she approached, she realized it was quite a small structure, only about the size of a single room, and small. About three quarters the height of a normal house, with a small door she would have to duck to go inside of.
“How queer,” she said aloud and knocked three times on the small door. 
“How queer indeed!” came a sudden reply, and it gave Amelia a jolt as she wasn’t expecting a reply so quickly, and she certainly wasn’t expecting it to come from what she saw before her. The small door, split level, swung open the top half revealing what seemed to be a … *ram. *
*“A talking ram,”* she blurted aloud.
“Indeed, but that doesn’t explain you.”
“I, well, I…” Amelia glanced around. She didn’t know much about who or what she was, just now that she had a box and he was a *ram. A talking ram.* “I, um, ahhh..”
“Um Ah? Is that some kind of name?” 
“No, I.. am Amelia,” she stammered. “I am not from here.”
“No shit,” replied the Ram, and kicked the remainder of the door open with his front hood, so he could trot out and see the girl now at his front door step. “You do look familiar though. Have any local relatives?”
Amelia furrowed her brow. “No, I… this box brought me here.” And she pressed the box outward, towards the Ram. Unexpectedly, the symbols along the perimeter glowed a bright and vibrant blue, throbbing in brightness. The box felt hot and vibrated. The sudden actions of the box shocked her, and she dropped it to the ground.  
“By the graces of the Elders,” the Ram gasped, taking several large steps away from the box. “So the legends are true.
“Pick it up and come inside,” the Ram replied, “we can speak over tea and warm fire.”

“No bed?” she asked.
“No need, I’m a Ram.” Amelia nodded, as if was obvious. “Do you like Earl Gray?” and the Ram poured her a glass with finesse Amelia didn’t think possible when using mostly your mouth as your primary tool.
“Thanks,” she said, and held the cup in her hands. While it wasn’t cold outside, the warmth still felt nice on her hands, and she blew the top, blowing a wave of hot steam in front of her. She was sure it formed a ram-like symbol, and it seemed normal because everything was so unnormal here.
“So,” the Ram said, after a period of silent awkwardness, “you have come to bring the box back to the Temple of [cool name]?”
“I wouldn’t say so much,” she said, “I don’t think I even know where I am, let alone enough to know where this box belongs.”
The Ram let out a long sigh, a mile long stare off into the distance. She could sense the disappointment in the sound, on his face. She never realized how expressive a Ram could be, but now she had a new respect for them, talking or not talking, and she felt bad that she’d made this talking one feel so terribly disappointed.
“Sorry to disappoint.”
The Ram nodded, licking a few droplets of his team before choosing to speak again. “It was a long shot that the box would be in the right hands.” He turned around, walking over to a small bookshelf. He nosed through his books before pulling out one and carrying it over in his mouth.
He dropped it on the table in front of her. *The Pylox Artifact*, the title said. Ne nudged it towards her. She reached her hands out and thumbed through the pages. They were old and reminded her of the envelope the mysterious woman had given her, the envelope she couldn’t find.
“Not too long ago, the sun would rise and fall,” the Ram began, “and with it the seasons changed and the Ego was well. The flowers grew and bloomed and died and life was as it should be.” 
Amelia nodded along, as it seemed at least the way nature worked was the same as it worked in her normal Earth world. 
“And each of the 12 were at one, there formed the bonds that tied the fabric of nature together. And at the heart of it all was a magnificent stone, the Invert Stone, a stone blacker then black. The stone was embeded into a large mountain side, and it was the apex of power. At some point the stone was broken, the how and the why had never been clear.
