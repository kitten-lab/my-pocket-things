"At his trial for impiety and corrupting youth, Socrates is quoted 'The unexamined life is not worth living'," the lecturer stated.

But what was there even to examine? Life beat the same drum, day in and day out, a slow dance to everyone's final reprieve--  death.

At least, that was the counter-thought Sam Munders had, half present for the lecture, half distracted by the bit of gum stuck to the bottom of her off-white generic white tennis shoes. She shoved her pencil into the groves, trying to angle the pink, sticky meat out. The lecture droned on in the background.

"Can anyone answer that? -- Samantha?"

Shit. She hadn't really been paying attention, distracted by her own apathy and fidgeting habits. She shook her head at him, shoulders shrugged.

"I would prefer you focus, Miss Munders, and not--" he gestured towards her, "--stab at your shoes." 

"I am sorry Mr.--" The pencil slipped into angle just right, a large wad of gum shooting across the lecture hall. One classmate's giggle broke the uncomfortable silence over the room. The professor was less amused.

"See me after class, Miss Munders."

--

It was a meaningless Tuesday, cloudy skies, Overall un-striking, meaningless in texture. Mr. Marlene was already buried in his laptop, presumably grading the papers from the night before. Or maybe playing solitaire. His bold, black rimmed glasses hung low on his nose, his eyes reading the screen above their aid.

Sam approached the professors desk with uncertainty. She briefly considered avenues of escape. She could tell him she got a call and her dorm was on fire. Or flooded. Or, she could just leave and maybe he wouldn't notice? It certainly wouldn't be the first time.

Before she could even collapse the escape-plan tree, he looked up at her. Just for a moment. He had that special look of pity in his eyes that adults seemed to have for her. Too bad it didn't make the world any less intolerable to exist in. 

His focus returned to the screen.

"Eh... uh.. you wanted to see me?" she asked, voice meek and flustered. Amplifying her voice felt like forcing all her breath through a too small straw.

"You're failing," Flat. Short. It landed like a knife in her chest. 

"I am doing my best." She shrugged, soothed herself.  What else did she have? The world expected more than what she could manage. The thought itself stung like shame.

Mr. Marlene seemed unmoved.

"You turn in nothing," he said flatly.

"What about the paper on Nietzsche?" she defended, "I turned that one in... I think."

With a deep sigh, Mr. Marlene closed his laptop, and looked up. The frown hidden in the subtle folds of his face was not lost on her.

"You did." Disappointment.

"... and you liked it?" Hope had a funny way of peeking out when Sam lead expected it. She didn't want to care what he thought, especially knowing she was already failing. But she did. She ultimately always did.

"That is the problem, Miss Munders," sighed the professor, walking around his desk as he spoke. "You are an excellent thinker -- one of my favorite voices in the class -- but that only shows when you bother to try. You've been warned by the school repeatedly. Your scholarship will only fund you for so long."

Sam lowered her eyes to the ground. 

"I know," she whispered. 

"Sam, knowing is only half the battle." Mr. Marlene placed one hand on her shoulder, a gesture of care. It grounded her. But it also made her skin crawl. 

"You also must do."

--

Sam caught a glimpse of herself in the mirror hung steady in the back of her small college dorm room. The only real word for her was bland. Boring. Basic. If understated had an opening for a mascot, they still wouldn't hire her. Much too dull to even qualify as an understatement.

Even her skin seems to take any light that hit it and turn it dull, sink it into the dark circles under her eyes, siphon it so completely only a pale gray cast remained. She looked sharply at herself in the mirror, pushing the stray hairs back from her face, straining to smile at herself.

The situation seemed grim. Her face flattened. She was failing all of her classes. She couldn't focus. The writing was on the wall: soon she'd have to go home, back to Uncle Chester's. Hopefully. He'd been gone for the last two years, ever since she'd gone off to college.

"When will you return?" she'd asked him, on the last day of that summer.

"When you do." he replied. He had always been so cryptic, both an antiques dealer and a master of puzzles.

Maybe he meant any time I do, she thought, a hopeful thought on a dreary day. Otherwise, where would she go? Certainly not to live with *them* again. Not that they'd welcome her any time soon. She'd find out soon enough, either way, if she couldn't pull her mind back together.

"Why can't you just *think*!" she grimaced into the mirror, poking her own self in the center of her forehead. "I mean, fuck!"

Never lacking a flare for the dramatics, especially when alone, she tossed the bookbag off her shoulders and flung herself backwards onto her bed. As she landed, something fell off the end of her bed, landing with a melodic thud on the ground nearby. She hadn't noticed anything where she got there, what was that?

--

The fallen object itself was plain, unassuming. A classic cardboard box used for shipping. Approximately two-foot squared, it was sealed with bright, glossy red electrical tape. She rolled it around in her hands, trying to find the side which constituted the top. Of course, it was on the bottom-side.

There, a simple label, typewriter font, read:

SAMANTHA MUNDERS
23 WINTER BLVD, 
ROSE CAMPUS, DORM 78
SOLA CITY

Each O was double-printed, creating a ghosted effect which made tiny Venn diagrams at each occurrence. 

What even is this, she wondered to herself. No one ever sent her mail. For a moment, her mind was finally freed from its constant hum of beige disappointment. It could be from anyone. In fact, it hardly appeared that it had been handled by any shipping company at all. There was no return address, no stamps, no postage paid. More than who sent this, she wondered how had it arrived here at all?

Maybe someone delivered it, she thought. But who? Sam was an introvert. Well, introvert is a gentle way to put anti-social. Friendless. A social pariah. 

It was more than being drained by social interactions. She instead found them debilitating, the endless banter, the social constructs. She found the whole dance to be strange and animal-natured, a rhythm she never learned, a song she couldn't sing. She'd found the best solution to her alienating experience of the world was to avoid engaging with anyone ever.

Fingernails picked at the red tape, catching the edge and tearing it quickly away. The cardboard offered no resistance, letting the skin of itself leave with the tape, coarse interior exposed in its wake. She grabbed both free flaps of the box, spread the box open and peered inside.

Packing peanuts. Green packing peanuts. God, how she hated packing peanuts. Maybe as much as she hated her peers. Both impossible to get rid of easily. Both a mess to have in your space.

She pushed them around along the top of the box. Beneath, a shimmer of new color: bright red in a perfect match the tape just torn away. Excited, she grabbed for it eagerly, packing peanuts overflowing the box and spilling onto the floor. She shakes her head. She'll be finding those for weeks, she felt sure.

A red envelope with a high gloss finish. The paper caught what little light pierced the room, shimmering its tiny specs of glitter. The effect was iridescent and silvery all at once, and the envelope shone brightly as if it wished to say 'the sun itself has come to place a spotlight on me'.

On the front, a perfectly penned Sam in dramatic cursive, ink somehow more luminous than the envelope itself. The silver was so intense, it almost gave a mirror effect, bouncing the colors all around it, while still somehow retaining its own light.

She turned it over. On the back, a wax seal pressed in black wax. The head of a raven, profile. The bird looks proud, head angled slightly up towards the heavens. Beneath that, three letters: CLM. Chester Lee Munders. Her uncle. She'd recognize his seal from anywhere. Her scalp tingled, small waves of goosebumps rolling through her skin.

"But how?" she said aloud, eagerness building in equal measure to a growing anxious edge. How did it get here? Where was her uncle now? She tossed the box to her left, landing it on its side. More fallen green soldiers scattered the bed and floor. 

"Unc?" she said, mostly kidding. He had always been so playful with her - in this rare times he had actually be home.

Perhaps this was a game too, and he was here to visit! Maybe, just maybe, he was under the bed? She dared a quick and childlike check.

"Gotcha!" but no one was there.

She inhaled deeply, body straightened again. Shoulder dropped. Obviously, that was a silly thought. She judged herself harshly for making such a choice. A foolish child, I am, she thought.

Her attention returned to the red envelope, still in her hands. All this looking for Chester when this letter ought explain things. She glanced around her desk, looking for an object she could use to slice inside.

She never liked the break the wax seals on any letters, they felt too important for that. Instead, she chose a pair of small sewing scissors she'd bought to cut thread for an embroidery piece she'd dreamt of starting, but hadn't yet "found the time for". 

She cut a notch into the corner, small enough to not catch the thick paper she could feel inside, but large enough to angle the little scissors in, cutting along the top seam. Inside, a thick stock, parchment-colored card. She squeezed the envelope. Just enough room to reach her fingers in and pull the note free.


Dear Samantha,

I am sending you this most important gift. Guard it with your life.
I cannot give you any more information then this:
Remember home.

When the Raven Calls,
Uncle Chester

Strange, she thought. It provided no information, but the whole message gave her an queasy feeling. The strange circumstances of the box kept piling up and what felt like a joyful moment was quickly feeling disconcerting, not expansive. 

Better than bored, she equally considered.

This left the question of what's in the box. What was this important gift?

She drove her hand into the box. At first, she felt nothing but the box's bottom. She fished further, tongue caught between teeth as she batted the packing material around. She almost gave up and turned the box out onto the but when her fingers scraped on something un-peanut-like at the bottom of the box. 

She grabbed at it. Pulled it quickly from the box. Peanuts spilled everywhere.

In her hands now was another box. Wooden, seemingly oak, with a simple hinge, and a simple brass clasp keeping it shut on it's front. Inlayed on its top, a scenic picture of a cabin in a mossy green woods. The feeling was very forest of the fae, she thought. The trees around the cabin snarled and curled, like they had their own stories to tell. The moss was on everything- the trees, the cabin, the fallen logs strewn in the yard, placed perfectly by the artist's sense of composition.

All in all, it was a beautiful box, but nothing about it screamed guard with your life.

She thumbed at the latch.
It wouldn't budge. Great. Stuck.
